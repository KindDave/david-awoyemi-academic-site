# Lesson 1.1: Composing an Original Song with GenAI

*GENAI 101 - Module 1: Music*

Draft narration script. Total running time about 3 min 3 s.

## Title slide

Lesson 1.1: Composing an Original Song with GenAI. GENAI 101 - Module 1: Music. Presented by Idowu David Awoyemi, The University of Alabama.

## Slide 2: What you will do

- Write short lyrics: two verses and a chorus
- Write a style prompt using the five-part frame
- Generate three versions, changing one variable each time
- Choose one using musical criteria and share it with a 75-word rationale

In this lesson you'll compose an original song. You'll write the lyrics yourself, two verses and a chorus, then write a style prompt, generate three versions of the song, and choose the strongest one using musical criteria. At the end you'll share it in the gallery with a short rationale explaining which prompt choices made the difference.

## Slide 3: A prompt is a brief, not a wish

- Vague: 'a nice pop song'
- Specific: 'upbeat indie pop, 118 BPM, bright acoustic guitar and handclaps, warm female alto, sparse verse building to a full chorus'
- Constraints are where style lives

Here's the core idea. A text-to-song tool takes a style prompt and your lyrics. If the prompt is vague, like 'a nice pop song', the model makes every musical decision for you and the result is generic. If the prompt is specific, like 'upbeat indie pop at one hundred eighteen beats per minute, bright acoustic guitar and handclaps, warm female alto, sparse first verse building to a full chorus', the model has constraints to work within. Constraints are where style lives.

## Slide 4: The five-part prompt frame

- Genre and era: what tradition?
- Tempo and feel: how fast, how does it move?
- Instrumentation: what do we hear?
- Vocal: who sings, and how?
- Arc and mood: how does it change, how should it feel?

Use the same five-part frame for every music prompt in this course. Genre and era: what tradition does this belong to? Tempo and feel: how fast is it, and how does it move? Instrumentation: what do we actually hear? Vocal: who sings, and how? And arc and mood: how does the song change over time, and how should it feel? Think of these as the six decisions a producer makes, minus the key, which most tools choose for you.

## Slide 5: Structure tags do the sequencing

- [Intro] [Verse 1] [Pre-Chorus] [Chorus] [Verse 2] [Chorus] [Bridge] [Outro]
- Without tags, choruses land in strange places
- Tagging the form is computational thinking: you sequence it so the tool can follow

Your lyrics need structure tags: verse one, chorus, verse two, bridge, and so on, in square brackets. The tags tell the model where sections begin. Without them, choruses land in odd places and the form falls apart. This is your first taste of computational thinking in the course. You are sequencing the form so a machine can follow it.

## Slide 6: Iterate on purpose

- Generation is cheap; judgment is not
- Change ONE variable per version
- Order: form first, then tempo and feel, then instrumentation, then vocal
- Score every version on the six-row evaluation grid

Generation is cheap. Judgment is not. When you regenerate, change one variable at a time so you can hear what it did. A good order is: get the form right first, then the tempo and feel, then the instrumentation, and finally the vocal. Score every version on the evaluation grid in the lesson page: does the form follow your tags, does the tempo match the brief, are the lyrics sung as written, is the vocal phrasing natural, and would you play this for a class? When you've chosen, post it in the gallery with your lyrics, your prompt, your rationale, and your AI credit. Have fun with it.
