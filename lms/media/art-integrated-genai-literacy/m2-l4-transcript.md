## Slide 2: The note you ignored

- Recall feedback on your creative work that you rightly rejected
- It misread the work, or it was so general it fit anything, or it asked for a different piece
- The same three tests apply to AI feedback

Think of a piece of feedback on your creative work that you rejected and were right to reject. What made you sure? Usually one of three things: the note misread the work, the note was so general it could apply to anything, or the note asked for a different piece than the one you were making. You'll use those same three tests on AI feedback today.

## Slide 3: AI feedback is fluent and unaccountable

- It has no stake in your scene and no memory of your rehearsal room
- It is a statistical average of what feedback usually looks like
- Useful for structure and cliches; harmful when it flattens a distinctive voice

A text model will produce feedback on anything you paste, instantly and confidently. It has no stake in your scene, no memory of your rehearsal room, and no way to know your students. Its feedback is a statistical average of what feedback usually looks like. That average can be genuinely useful for spotting structural problems and cliches. It is useless, or harmful, when it pushes a distinctive voice toward the mean.

## Slide 4: Three classes of feedback

- Useful: points to a line, names a verifiable problem, fits your intent
- Generic: 'raise the stakes', 'show don't tell', with nowhere to point
- Wrong: misreads the text, invents a detail, pushes toward a different scene
- Classify all five notes with textual evidence

Classify every note into one of three classes. Useful feedback points to a specific line, names a problem you can verify by rereading, and suggests a change consistent with your intent. Generic feedback could be said about any scene: raise the stakes, develop the characters, show don't tell. Ask the model to locate it in the text; if it can't, discard it. Wrong feedback misreads the text, invents a detail, or pushes the scene toward a different one. You'll classify five notes and cite the text as evidence for each.

## Slide 5: Prompting for better feedback

- Specify the lens: 'read as a dramaturg preparing first-rehearsal notes'
- Specify the criteria: 'comment only on objective, obstacle, and subtext'
- Specify the form: 'five notes, each quoting the line it refers to'
- Then ask: 'Which notes are you least confident about, and why?'

Feedback quality depends on the request. Specify the lens, the criteria, and the form. Then ask a follow-up: which of these notes are you least confident about, and why? Models are often more honest about uncertainty when asked directly. Test one useful note in a rewrite and keep both versions; you'll post the before and after.

## Slide 6: Would you stage it?

- Authorship: who is credited when a model drafted 40 percent of the lines?
- Consent: did the playwrights in the training data agree? Did your students?
- Representation: models reproduce the biases of their training data
- Labor: which theater jobs are you automating?
- Disclosure: does the audience know? Should they?

Finally, the ethics of putting AI-assisted work in front of an audience. Five questions. Authorship: who is credited when a model drafted forty percent of the lines? Consent: did the writers whose plays trained the model agree, and did your students agree to have their improvisations fed into a tool? Representation: models reproduce the biases of their training data, and AI-generated characters from underrepresented communities can default to stereotype. Labor: which jobs are you automating? Disclosure: does the audience know, and should they? There is no single right answer. There is one wrong one: not asking. Write your position, print the credit line you'd put in the program, and argue it out in the forum.
