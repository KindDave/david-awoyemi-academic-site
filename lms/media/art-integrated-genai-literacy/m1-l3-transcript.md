# Lesson 1.3: Scoring a Scene with AI-Generated Soundtracks

*GENAI 101 - Module 1: Music*

Draft narration script. Total running time about 3 min 11 s.

## Title slide

Lesson 1.3: Scoring a Scene with AI-Generated Soundtracks. GENAI 101 - Module 1: Music. Presented by Idowu David Awoyemi, The University of Alabama.

## Slide 2: What you will do

- Write a cue sheet for a 30 to 60 second scene with two contrasting cues
- Generate two candidates per cue and score all four on six criteria
- Diagnose the weakest cue and apply one of three fixes
- Assemble the scored scene and share it

In this lesson you become a film composer for an hour. You'll pick a short scene, write a cue sheet with two contrasting cues, generate candidates for each cue, score them on six criteria, fix the weakest one, and assemble the scored scene. This is the lesson where the AI will fail you most often, and that's the point: the competencies here are problem solving and evaluation.

## Slide 3: Scoring starts with a cue sheet

- For each cue: start and end time, purpose, mood, tempo, instrumentation, shape
- Purpose: establish a place, reveal a feeling, build tension, or release it
- The cue sheet is both the prompt source and the evaluation standard

Before you touch a tool, write a cue sheet. For each cue, note when it starts and ends, what it needs to do, and its mood, tempo, instrumentation, and shape. Does this cue establish a place, reveal a feeling, build tension, or release it? A cue sheet turns 'make it sad' into a brief the model can act on, and it gives you a standard to evaluate against afterward. That double role is why we always write it first.

## Slide 4: Evaluating AI output is a skill

- Mood match. Tempo and energy. Instrumentation.
- Structure. Technical quality. Fit under dialogue.
- Rate each 1 to 4. A 1 or a 2 is a problem to solve, not a reason to quit.

'I like it' is not evaluation. Evaluation compares the output with the brief on named criteria. For a cue, there are six: mood match, tempo and energy, instrumentation, structure, technical quality, and fit under dialogue. Rate each from one to four. Anything scoring a one or a two is a problem to solve, not a reason to give up. You'll score four candidate cues this way, and the grid you fill in becomes part of your gallery post.

## Slide 5: Three fixes, in order

- 1. Revise the prompt: add a reference, a tempo, and a shape
- 2. Change the tool or mode: instrumental mode, or a mood-based tool like Soundraw
- 3. Edit the audio: trim, fade, layer, or slow it in Soundtrap
- Diagnosing which fix applies is the problem-solving competency

When a cue misses, work through three fixes in order. First, revise the prompt. Most misses come from an underspecified mood or tempo, so add a reference, a tempo, and a shape, like 'starts sparse, swells at twenty seconds'. Second, change the tool or the mode. If the model keeps adding vocals, switch to instrumental mode; if it can't do the genre, try a mood-based tool. Third, edit the audio yourself. Sometimes the best fix is a twelve-second crossfade you make by hand. Diagnosing which fix applies is the skill.

## Slide 6: Playing against the scene

- Watch the warm-up clip with the sound off, then on
- A score can confirm the scene, add to it, or contradict it
- Make that choice deliberately for your second cue

One more idea. In the warm-up you'll watch a scene with the sound off and guess what's happening, then watch it with the sound on. A score can confirm what you see, add something to it, or contradict it. Film composers call that last option 'playing against the scene'. When you write your second cue, make that choice on purpose. Then assemble your scene, export it, and post it with the cue sheet, your scores, and a rationale about the fix you chose. I'll be listening for the transition between your two cues.
