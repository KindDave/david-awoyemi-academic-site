# Lesson 1.4: Voice, Ownership, and Ethics in AI Music

*GENAI 101 - Module 1: Music*

Draft narration script. Total running time about 3 min 32 s.

## Title slide

Lesson 1.4: Voice, Ownership, and Ethics in AI Music. GENAI 101 - Module 1: Music. Presented by Idowu David Awoyemi, The University of Alabama.

## Slide 2: Whose song is it?

- Who contributed to the song you made in Lesson 1.1?
- You, the model, the artists whose recordings trained it, the company, the mixing tool
- Who should be credited? Who should be paid? Who was asked?

Think about the song you made in lesson one point one. Who contributed to it? You wrote the lyrics and the brief. The model generated the melody and vocals. The people whose recordings trained the model contributed too, whether they knew it or not. So did the company that built the tool. Now ask three questions: who should be credited, who should be paid, and who was asked? Those questions are this lesson.

## Slide 3: Four questions every AI music project raises

- Training data and consent: whose work taught the model?
- Voice and likeness: prompt for a type of voice, never a real person
- Ownership and copyright: human contribution can be protected; raw generation may not be
- Disclosure: audiences, students, and collaborators deserve to know

There are four. First, training data and consent. Text-to-music models learned from large collections of recordings, and in many cases the artists were neither asked nor paid. Second, voice and likeness. Models can imitate a recognizable singer, and several jurisdictions now treat that as a violation of a person's rights. The course rule is simple: prompt for a type of voice, never a named person. Third, ownership. In the United States, the Copyright Office has said that purely AI-generated work is not protectable, while your own creative contribution can be. Fourth, disclosure. That's why every artifact in this course carries a credit line.

## Slide 4: A framework for taking a position

- Stakeholders: who is affected?
- Harms and benefits: what does each gain or lose?
- Consent: was permission sought?
- Transparency: was the AI role disclosed?
- Alternatives: could the goal be reached with less harm?
- Position: what should have happened, and what rule would you set?

You'll analyze one real case using a six-step framework. Identify the stakeholders. Weigh the harms and benefits to each. Ask whether consent was sought. Ask whether the AI's role was disclosed. Consider whether the same goal could have been reached with less harm. Then take a position: what should have happened, and what rule would you set in your own classroom? One sentence per step is enough. Reasonable people will land in different places; what matters is that your reasoning is visible.

## Slide 5: Text models are not legal counsel

- Ask a text model to summarize your case; expect a fluent, confident answer
- Mark every claim: confirmed, unconfirmed, or wrong, against two real sources
- Models blend jurisdictions, go out of date, and sometimes invent cases

Part of the activity is asking a text model to summarize the legal and ethical issues in your case. You'll get a fluent, confident answer. Then you'll check it against two sources you found yourself, one news report and one primary source, and mark every claim as confirmed, unconfirmed, or wrong. Models blend jurisdictions, go out of date, and occasionally invent court cases. Counting what you couldn't confirm is the AI feedback evaluation competency in action, and it's a habit you'll want your students to have.

## Slide 6: Your attribution statement

- Lyrics written by [name]. Melody, arrangement, and vocals generated with [tool, version] from those lyrics and a style prompt by [name].
- Final mix edited by [name] in [tool]. This work includes AI-generated material.
- Post it with your case analysis and the rule you would set for your classroom

Finally, you'll write an attribution statement for your own song, naming the human and the machine contributions and disclosing that AI-generated material is included. A template is on the lesson page. Post it in the forum with your case analysis, your count of confirmed and unconfirmed claims, and the rule you'd set for AI music in your classroom. Then find two classmates who took a different position and engage with their reasoning, not just their conclusion. This is the debate that will follow you into every school you teach in.
