# How to Analyze an Instructional Design Case

*ID 553 - Module 2: ID Case Analysis I*

Draft narration script. Total running time about 5 min 46 s.

## Title slide

How to Analyze an Instructional Design Case. ID 553 - Module 2: ID Case Analysis I. Presented by Idowu David Awoyemi, The University of Alabama.

## Slide 2: Why cases?

- Models tell you the steps; they do not tell you what to do when the client wants a course but needs a job aid
- Case analysis develops judgment
- A case is realistic, incomplete, and messy: more information than you need, less than you want
- The goal is a defensible analysis, not the right answer

Instructional design is a profession of judgment. Models tell you what steps to take. They don't tell you what to do when the client wants a course but needs a job aid, when the subject-matter expert won't return your calls, or when the budget disappears halfway through. Case analysis develops the judgment that models can't supply. A case is a realistic, incomplete, messy account of a design situation, with more information than you need and less than you want. Your task isn't to find the right answer, because there usually isn't one. It's to construct a defensible analysis.

## Slide 3: Step 1: Summarize the situation

- One paragraph: who is involved, what is being asked, what has happened so far
- No interpretation yet
- A reader who has not seen the case should understand the setup

Step one: summarize the situation in one paragraph. Who's involved, what's being asked, what's happened so far. Resist interpreting yet. Here's a short example we'll follow through all six steps. A school district asks a designer for an online course on classroom management for first-year teachers, because new-teacher turnover is high. Summary: district leadership requests a course; the stated driver is turnover; the audience is first-year teachers.

## Slide 4: Step 2: Stakeholders and interests

- Everyone with a stake: what they want, what they fear
- Include the learners; they are often absent from the case's conversations
- Note where interests conflict

Step two: list every stakeholder, what they want, and what they fear. In our example: district leaders want turnover down and want something visible; principals want fewer discipline referrals and don't want their teachers pulled out of class; first-year teachers want to survive the year and fear being judged; mentors have the tacit knowledge and no time; students, who never appear in the request, want calm classrooms. Note the conflicts. Leadership wants a course they can point to. Teachers need support in the moment, not a module in August.

## Slide 5: Step 3: Frame the design problem

- The presenting request is rarely the problem
- Is there a performance gap? Is it knowledge, or tools, incentives, process, environment?
- Which constraints are fixed, and which are negotiable?
- What is unknown that matters, and how could you find out?

Step three is the heart of the analysis: frame the problem. The presenting request, 'build us a course', is rarely the problem. Is there a performance gap, and is it caused by a lack of knowledge or by something else? In our example: exit interviews might show new teachers leave because of isolation and unmanageable class sizes, not because they never learned management techniques. If so, a course addresses the wrong cause. State the problem in one or two sentences naming the gap, the learners, and the constraint that makes it hard. And list what you don't know yet and how you'd find out.

## Slide 6: Step 4: Generate and weigh alternatives

- At least three: the obvious one, a non-instructional one, and one a novice would not think of
- For each: benefits, costs, risks, and who would favor or resist it
- Example: the course; a mentoring structure with release time; a just-in-time library plus monthly problem-solving circles

Step four: propose at least three courses of action. One should be the obvious one the client asked for. One should be non-instructional or only partly instructional. One should be something a less experienced designer wouldn't think of. In the example: the online course; a structured mentoring program with release time; or a short just-in-time video library paired with monthly problem-solving circles led by mentors. For each, list benefits, costs, risks, and which stakeholders would favor or resist it. Leadership likes the course. Principals like the circles. The budget office dislikes release time.

## Slide 7: Steps 5 and 6: Recommend, then anticipate

- Choose one alternative or a sequence; justify with the problem, stakeholders, constraints, and a named principle or model
- Say what you would do in the first two weeks
- What could go wrong? What early indicators would you watch? What if it fails?

Step five: recommend and justify. Choose one alternative, or a sequence, and justify it by reference to the problem statement, the stakeholders, the constraints, and at least one principle or model from the course. Say what you'd do in the first two weeks. Step six: anticipate consequences. What could go wrong? What early indicators would tell you it's working? What would you do if it fails? In the example, a recommendation might be the circles plus library first, with a needs assessment in the first two weeks to test the isolation hypothesis, and referral data and attendance as early indicators.

## Slide 8: Common mistakes

- Solving the presenting request
- Ignoring the learners
- Treating every constraint as fixed
- Recommending without trade-offs: if there is no downside, look harder
- Retelling the case instead of analyzing it

Five mistakes sink most first analyses. Solving the presenting request: the client asked for a course, so you design a course. Ignoring the learners: cases are told from the designer's or client's side, so you have to ask what learners know and need. Treating constraints as fixed when many are negotiable, as long as you say how you'd negotiate. Recommending without trade-offs: if your recommendation has no downside, you haven't looked hard enough. And retelling the case. Summarize briefly; spend your words on analysis.

## Slide 9: What a strong analysis looks like

- Specific to the case; quotes or paraphrases what people said and what the data showed
- Applies course concepts by name
- Reads like a memo a designer would write for a client
- 1,500 to 2,000 words; takes a position and defends it

A strong analysis is specific to the case and cites evidence from it. It applies course concepts by name. It reads like a memo a designer would actually hand to a client. It runs about fifteen hundred to two thousand words, takes a position, and defends it while acknowledging what a reasonable colleague might do differently. Before you read the Crestview case, take a situation from your own work where someone asked for training and run it through steps two and three in ten minutes. Bring it to the discussion.
