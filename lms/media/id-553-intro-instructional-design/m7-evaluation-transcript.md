# Formative and Summative Evaluation

*ID 553 - Module 7: Evaluation*

Draft narration script. Total running time about 6 min 39 s.

## Title slide

Formative and Summative Evaluation. ID 553 - Module 7: Evaluation. Presented by Idowu David Awoyemi, The University of Alabama.

## Slide 2: Two questions, two evaluations

- Formative: how can this instruction be improved before it is finished? Audience: the design team
- Summative: did the finished instruction work, and is it worth continuing? Audience: decision makers
- Both are part of the design, not afterthoughts
- Every plan should say when and how both will happen

Evaluation answers two different questions. Formative evaluation asks how the instruction can be improved before it's finished; it happens during design and development, and its audience is the design team. Summative evaluation asks whether the finished instruction worked and whether it's worth continuing; it happens after implementation, and its audience is the people who fund and mandate the instruction. Both are part of the design. Dick and Carey give each its own component and make revision a loop that formative evaluation feeds.

## Slide 3: A real module: the starting point

- A 25-minute e-learning module on barcode medication administration, four objectives
- Objective 3: decide whether a mismatch alert requires stopping
- Built from the storyboard; never yet seen by a nurse
- The plan: one-to-one with three nurses, then small group, then field trial on one unit

Let me walk you through a formative evaluation of a real module: a twenty-five-minute e-learning module on barcode medication administration with four objectives. Objective three is the decision about mismatch alerts. It was built from a storyboard and had never been seen by a nurse. The plan was Dick and Carey's three stages: one-to-one evaluation with three nurses, then a small group, then a field trial on one unit.

## Slide 4: One-to-one evaluation

- Three learners representing the range: one experienced with the vendor system, one average, one anxious about technology
- Sit beside them; think aloud; note errors, confusions, pacing; give the posttest
- Findings: the alert screenshot was from an older version; the override step said 'MRN' with no expansion; the anxious nurse took 41 minutes
- Catches the gross errors: wrong content, unclear instructions, missing steps

One-to-one: three nurses, one at a time, chosen to span the range of the population. One had used the vendor's system elsewhere, one was typical, one described herself as not a computer person. I sat beside each of them, asked them to think aloud, noted errors and confusions and pacing, and gave the posttest at the end. Findings: the alert screenshot was from an older software version. The override step used the abbreviation MRN with no expansion, and two of three didn't know it. And the anxious nurse took forty-one minutes for a module planned at twenty-five. This stage catches the gross errors: wrong content, unclear instructions, missing steps.

## Slide 5: Small-group evaluation

- After revision: twelve nurses, conditions close to real use, designer observing but not intervening
- Pretest, posttest, item-level results, time on task, short attitude survey
- Objectives 1, 2, 4: 85 to 100 percent posttest success
- Objective 3: 58 percent. Learners had seen one alert example and no practice under time pressure
- Revision: two practice scenarios with feedback before the posttest items

After revising, I ran a small group: twelve nurses under conditions close to real use, with me observing but not intervening. I collected a pretest, a posttest, item-level results, time on task, and a short attitude survey. Objectives one, two, and four came in between eighty-five and one hundred percent posttest success. Objective three came in at fifty-eight percent. The item-by-objective table made the cause obvious: learners had seen a single alert example and had no practice making the decision under any pressure. The revision was two practice scenarios with feedback, inserted before the posttest items. The overall score had looked fine. Analyzing by objective is what found the problem.

## Slide 6: Field trial

- Revised module deployed to one full unit, 34 nurses, through the LMS, designer absent
- Same data as small group, plus facilitator feedback from the unit educator
- Objective 3 rose to 88 percent
- New finding: the LMS launched the module in a window too small for the simulation; a technical fix, invisible until real conditions
- Field trials test implementation: guides, logistics, technology, the real context

Then the field trial: the revised module deployed to one full unit, thirty-four nurses, through the LMS, with me absent. Same data as the small group, plus feedback from the unit educator who facilitated it. Objective three rose to eighty-eight percent. And a new problem appeared that no earlier stage could have found: the LMS launched the module in a window too small for the simulation, so learners scrolled inside a scrolling frame. A technical fix, invisible until real conditions. That's what the field trial is for: it tests implementation, the facilitator guides, the logistics, the technology, and the effect of the real context.

## Slide 7: Expert review and using the data

- Alongside learner stages: a subject-matter expert checks accuracy, a second designer checks alignment, an accessibility reviewer checks conformance
- Build an item-by-objective table; compare pre and post by objective, not only overall
- Low success with adequate time: a strategy problem. Low success with little time: a coverage problem
- Pair every problem with a location and a proposed revision; keep a revision log

Alongside the learner-based stages, run expert reviews: a subject-matter expert for accuracy, a second designer for alignment and strategy, an accessibility reviewer for conformance. Expert review is fast and finds things learners can't name. Then use the data properly. Build an item-by-objective table. Compare pretest and posttest by objective, not only overall, because a high overall gain can hide one objective nobody learned. Low success with adequate instruction time points to a strategy problem; low success with little time points to a coverage problem. Pair every problem with a location in the instruction and a proposed revision, and keep a revision log.

## Slide 8: Summative evaluation: Kirkpatrick's four levels

- Reaction: engaging, relevant, satisfactory? Specific survey items
- Learning: knowledge, skills, attitudes, confidence? Posttest, performance assessment, pre-post
- Behavior: applied on the job? Observation, supervisor reports, system data at 30 to 90 days
- Results: did the targeted outcomes change? Error rates, help desk calls, retention, cost
- Level 1 alone says almost nothing; level 4 needs a baseline or comparison

Summative evaluation judges the instruction against its purpose, and the classic framework is Kirkpatrick's four levels. Reaction: did learners find it engaging, relevant, and satisfactory? Learning: did they acquire the intended knowledge, skills, attitudes, and confidence? Behavior: do they apply it on the job, measured by observation, supervisor reports, or system data at thirty to ninety days? Results: did the targeted outcomes change, such as error rates or help desk calls? Two cautions. Level one alone tells you almost nothing about effectiveness, because satisfaction and learning are only weakly related. And level four outcomes are affected by many things besides instruction, so claim contribution rather than sole cause, and design in a baseline or a comparison.

## Slide 9: Designing your evaluation plan

- Formative: which stages, participants, instruments, data, and decision rules for revision
- Summative: which levels and why, instruments and timing, baseline or comparison, who gets the report
- Logistics and ethics: who does what, when, at what cost; consent, privacy, storage
- Reporting: the form of the report and the decision it informs
- A plan you can actually carry out beats an ambitious one that will not happen

For your project, the evaluation plan should specify four things. Formative: which stages you'll run, with participants, instruments, data, and the decision rules you'll use to decide what to revise. Summative: the levels you'll measure and why, the instruments and timing, the baseline or comparison strategy, and who receives the report and what decision it informs. Logistics and ethics: who does what, when, at what cost, and what happens to the data. And reporting. One rule above all: an evaluation plan that can actually be carried out with the resources available beats an ambitious one that will never happen. Bring your draft to the peer review exchange, and remember that peer review is itself an expert-review stage of formative evaluation.
