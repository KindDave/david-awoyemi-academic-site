## Slide 2: Why prototype

- A design document describes instruction; a prototype lets people experience it
- Stakeholders who approve a document often reject the module built from it
- Prototypes surface reactions early, when changes are cheap
- They expose what the designer cannot see from inside: confusing interactions, fake scenarios, pages that run long

A design document describes instruction. A prototype lets people experience it. Stakeholders who approve a document often reject the module built from it, because the document never made them imagine what learners would actually do. Prototypes surface those reactions early, when changes are cheap. They also expose problems the designer can't see from the inside: an interaction that's confusing, a scenario that feels fake, a page that takes three times longer than planned. Prototyping is the core of SAM, and it fits inside ADDIE and Dick and Carey as the front half of formative evaluation.

## Slide 3: One interaction, four fidelities: paper

- The interaction: a mismatch alert appears; the learner chooses stop, override, or proceed
- Paper: three index cards, one per choice, with the consequence written on the back
- Read the scenario aloud; the tester picks a card and flips it
- Tests: sequence, plausibility, whether the consequence makes sense. Cost: twenty minutes

Let's follow one interaction through four levels of fidelity. The interaction: a mismatch alert appears during medication administration, and the learner must choose to stop, override, or proceed. At paper fidelity, that's three index cards, one per choice, with the consequence written on the back. You read the scenario aloud, the tester picks a card and flips it. Twenty minutes to make. It tests whether the sequence makes sense, whether the scenario is plausible, and whether the consequences feel right. In my first test, a nurse said the override option would never be offered that early. The cards changed before anything was built.

## Slide 4: Low, medium, and high fidelity

- Low: clickable slides with placeholder graphics; the three choices are buttons linking to consequence slides. Tests interaction logic and wording. Hours
- Medium: an authoring-tool build with real text, draft graphics, synthetic narration. Tests look, timing, usability. Days
- High: near-final with produced media. Tests polish, technical function, accessibility. Weeks
- Start at the lowest fidelity that answers your current question

Low fidelity is clickable slides: placeholder graphics, the three choices as buttons linking to consequence slides. A few hours. It tests the interaction logic and the wording of the choices. Medium fidelity is an authoring-tool build with real text, draft graphics, and synthetic narration; a few days, and it tests look and feel, timing, and usability. High fidelity is near-final with produced media, and tests polish, technical function, and accessibility conformance. The rule: start at the lowest fidelity that can answer your current question, and move up only when the lower version stops producing useful findings. Test the flow on paper before you write a single line in an authoring tool.

## Slide 5: Storyboards

- The handoff document between designer and developer, and the artifact stakeholders review
- Per screen: ID tied to objective, Gagne event, visual, on-screen text, narration, interaction, feedback, navigation, accessibility notes, development notes
- 15 to 40 rows for a 20-minute module
- A table in a document or spreadsheet is enough

A storyboard describes each screen: what the learner sees, hears, and does, and what happens next. It's the handoff between designer and developer and the main artifact for stakeholder review. Each row carries a screen ID tied to the lesson and objective, the Gagne event the screen serves, the visual, the exact on-screen text, the narration script with a note on whether it replaces or supplements text, the interaction and branching rules, the feedback for each response, navigation, accessibility notes such as alt text and keyboard alternatives, and development notes. A twenty-minute module runs fifteen to forty rows. A table in a document or spreadsheet is enough.

## Slide 6: Branching scenarios

- Decision practice is where most e-learning fails to go beyond click-next
- Situation, decision, consequence, continue
- Design from the task analysis: every branch point is a decision screen; recorded errors are the wrong options
- Three or four decisions, two or three options each, a few converging paths
- Prototype in Twine or on index cards first

Decision practice is where most e-learning fails to go beyond click-next. A branching scenario presents a situation, asks the learner to decide, shows the consequence, and continues. Design it from your task analysis: every branch point in the procedural analysis becomes a decision screen, and the common errors you recorded become the wrong options. Keep it manageable: three or four decisions with two or three options each and a few converging paths. Prototype it in Twine or on cards before you build it.

## Slide 7: Testing a prototype

- Explain that you are testing the design, not the person
- Ask them to work through it, thinking aloud
- Do not help unless they are stuck for more than a minute; note where and why
- Afterward: what was confusing, too easy, too hard; can they perform the objective now?
- Three one-to-one tests find most of the serious problems

Even a paper prototype should be tested with someone who resembles the learners. A one-to-one test takes thirty to forty-five minutes. Explain that you're testing the design, not the person. Ask them to work through the prototype, thinking aloud. Don't help unless they're stuck for more than a minute, and note where and why. Afterward, ask what was confusing, what was too easy or too hard, what they'd change, and whether they can perform the objective now. Record the findings and decide what changes before the next iteration. Three one-to-one tests find most of the serious problems.

## Slide 8: Working with subject-matter experts, and your assignment

- Give the SME a specific task with a deadline, not an open request
- Explain design decisions in terms of learner performance
- Separate accuracy review (theirs) from design review (yours)
- Prototype early so the SME reacts to something concrete
- Assignment 7: a complete storyboard for one lesson and a low-fidelity prototype of the interaction with the most decision practice

Most designers build content with a subject-matter expert. Give them a specific task with a deadline: review this storyboard for accuracy, or provide three realistic error cases. Explain your design decisions in terms of learner performance so they understand why the module isn't a lecture. Separate accuracy review, which is their call, from design review, which is yours. And prototype early so they react to something concrete instead of a document. For assignment seven, you'll produce a complete storyboard for one lesson and a low-fidelity prototype of at least one interaction. Choose the lesson that best represents your strategy and the interaction that carries the most decision practice.
