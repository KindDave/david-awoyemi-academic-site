## Slide 2: What task analysis is for

- What, exactly, must the learner be able to do, and what does that performance consist of?
- Turns a goal into a map of knowledge, skills, and decisions
- The map determines objectives, sequence, strategies, and assessments
- Without it, designers teach what they know rather than what the performance requires

Task analysis answers one question: what, exactly, must the learner be able to do, and what does that performance consist of? It turns a goal like 'nurses will administer medications safely in the new system' into a map of the knowledge, skills, and decisions the goal requires. That map determines your objectives, your sequence, your strategies, and your assessments. Without it, designers teach what they happen to know rather than what the performance actually requires.

## Slide 3: Step 1: Classify the goal (Gagne's outcome types)

- Verbal information: state, recall. Cluster analysis
- Intellectual skills: discriminate, classify, apply rules, solve problems. Hierarchical analysis
- Cognitive strategies: manage one's own thinking
- Motor skills: perform a physical procedure. Procedural analysis
- Attitudes: choose to act a certain way
- Most real goals combine types

Start by classifying the goal using Gagne's outcome types, because the type determines the analysis method and, later, the instructional conditions. Verbal information is stating and recalling; you analyze it by clustering. Intellectual skills are discriminating, classifying, applying rules, and solving problems; you analyze them hierarchically. Cognitive strategies are how learners manage their own thinking. Motor skills are physical procedures; you analyze them procedurally. Attitudes are choices. Most real goals combine types: a procedure with decisions at branch points and information needed at several steps.

## Slide 4: Procedural analysis, worked live

- Goal: administer a medication with bedside barcode scanning
- 1. Scan the wristband. 1.1 Match: proceed. 1.2 No read: rescan; after two failures, manual override. 1.3 Override: enter record number, confirm two identifiers, document reason
- 2. Scan the medication. 2.1 Mismatch alert: stop; follow alert workflow
- Branch points are where errors happen and where practice concentrates

Let's do a procedural analysis live. The goal: administer a medication with bedside barcode scanning. Step one, scan the patient's wristband. If it reads and matches, proceed. If it doesn't read, rescan; after two failures, use the manual override. The override is its own sub-procedure: enter the record number, confirm two identifiers verbally, document the reason. Step two, scan the medication barcode. If a mismatch alert appears, stop, do not administer, follow the alert workflow. Notice what the analysis exposed: the branch points. That's where errors happen, where practice should concentrate, and where the expert performs automatically and would never think to teach.

## Slide 5: Hierarchical analysis, worked live

- Ask repeatedly: 'What must the learner already be able to do in order to learn this?'
- Decide whether a mismatch alert requires stopping
-   requires: interpret the alert type
-   requires: distinguish wrong-patient, wrong-drug, wrong-time alerts
-   requires: read the alert display (entry skill)
- Stop when you reach what learners already have

Now a hierarchical analysis for an intellectual skill. Take the decision: does a mismatch alert require stopping? Ask: what must the learner already be able to do in order to learn this? They must interpret the alert type. Ask again: to interpret the type, they must distinguish wrong-patient, wrong-drug, and wrong-time alerts. Ask again: to distinguish them, they must read the alert display, which is an entry skill. Each answer becomes a subordinate skill below the one above, and lower skills must be learned first. You stop when you reach what learners already have.

## Slide 6: Cluster analysis and cognitive task analysis

- Verbal information: group items into meaningful clusters; clusters often become job aids
- Experts make decisions they cannot articulate
- CTA: structured interviews, think-aloud, critical incidents surface cues, decisions, and novice errors
- Even a light version improves any analysis; instruction built on CTA outperforms expert self-report

Two more methods. For verbal information, cluster the items into meaningful sets by function, location, or time; clusters often turn directly into job aids. And for complex tasks, cognitive task analysis. Experts make decisions they can't easily articulate. Structured interviews, think-aloud protocols, and critical-incident recall surface the cues experts attend to, the decisions they make, and the errors novices make at the same points. Full cognitive task analysis is expensive, but even a light version, asking an expert to narrate a recent difficult case and probing at each decision, improves any analysis. The research is clear that instruction built on it outperforms instruction built on expert self-report.

## Slide 7: The entry-skill line and checking the analysis

- Draw a line: below is assumed, above is taught. The line comes from the learner analysis
- Two subgroups may need two lines and two paths
- Completeness: could a novice perform the goal using only what is here?
- Accuracy: observe the task; documentation misses workarounds
- Every branch point explicit, with common errors noted

Draw a line across the bottom of your analysis. Everything below it is assumed; everything above it is taught. Where you draw the line comes from the learner analysis, and if learners vary widely, you may need two lines and two paths. Then check the analysis. Completeness: could a novice perform the goal using only what's here? Have an expert and a novice review it. Accuracy: observe the task performed, because analyses built from documentation miss workarounds and tacit steps. And make every branch point explicit with its common errors; those errors become practice items and assessment distractors.

## Slide 8: Common mistakes and Assignment 6

- Listing topics instead of performances
- Stopping decomposition too early
- Teaching the happy path with no decisions or error handling
- Analyzing from documentation without observing
- Assignment 6: classify one goal, decompose to three levels, mark the entry line, have a performer review it

The common mistakes: listing topics instead of performances; stopping too early so the first objective is unteachable in one lesson; teaching the happy path with no decisions or error handling; and analyzing from documentation without observing anyone. For assignment six, choose one goal from your project, classify it, decompose it with the right method to at least three levels, number every element, mark the entry-skill line using your learner analysis, and have at least one person who performs the task review it. Then write one paragraph on what the analysis revealed that you would not have taught from your own knowledge. That paragraph is usually the most interesting thing in the assignment.
