## Slide 2: Why analyze learners?

- Every design decision assumes something about learners
- Wrong assumptions make instruction fail no matter how well built
- Learner analysis replaces assumptions with evidence; context analysis does the same for settings
- Skipping it is the most common cause of accurate, well-produced, useless instruction

Every design decision assumes something about the learners: what they already know, what they can attend to, what motivates them, what tools they have, and how much time they'll give you. If those assumptions are wrong, the instruction fails no matter how well it's built. Learner analysis replaces assumptions with evidence. Context analysis does the same for the settings where learning and performance happen. Skipping this step is the most common cause of instruction that is accurate, beautifully produced, and useless.

## Slide 3: Two analyses of the same population

- From assumption: 'Nurses are busy adults who dislike computers; keep it short and simple'
- From data: a quarter have used the vendor system before; night shift will not attend daytime classes; 61 percent of past help calls hit five workflows; veterans report anxiety, novices report boredom
- The designs diverge: one course for everyone versus a test-out path, shift-specific support, and five priority workflows

Here's why it matters. Two designers analyze the same four hundred nurses. The first works from assumption: nurses are busy adults who dislike computers, so keep the training short and simple. The second collects data: a quarter of the nurses have used the vendor's system at a previous employer; the night shift will not attend daytime classes; sixty-one percent of last time's help desk calls concerned five workflows; veteran nurses report anxiety and younger nurses report boredom with basics. The first designer builds one course for everyone. The second builds a test-out path, shift-specific support, and practice focused on five workflows. Same population, completely different designs.

## Slide 4: What to find out

- Entry skills and prior knowledge, including misconceptions
- Attitudes toward the content, the delivery mode, and the organization
- Motivation: why are they here, and what are the consequences?
- Educational and ability levels: reading, language, digital skills
- Group characteristics: size, heterogeneity, relationships
- For adults: competing demands, relevant experience, expected autonomy

What should you find out? Entry skills and prior knowledge, including misconceptions, because prior knowledge is the strongest predictor of new learning. Attitudes toward the content, toward the delivery mode, and toward the organization, because attitudes explain resistance. Motivation: why are they here, and what happens if they learn or don't? Educational and ability levels, including reading, language, and digital skills. Group characteristics. And for adults, competing demands on time, experience you can leverage, and the autonomy they expect. One caution: learning preferences can inform engagement, but the idea of matching instruction to learning styles has no empirical support. Don't design on it.

## Slide 5: How to find out

- Interviews: ask about the task, not about training. 'Walk me through the last time you did this.'
- Observation: watch for workarounds
- Surveys: short, specific, for breadth
- Existing data: performance records, help desk logs, error reports. Most objective, least used
- Triangulate. When self-report and performance data disagree, believe the data and ask why

How do you find out? Use more than one method, because each has blind spots. Interview a sample of learners and their supervisors, and ask about the task rather than about training: walk me through the last time you did this. Observe learners performing the task, and watch for workarounds; they reveal what the current process fails to support. Survey for breadth, keeping it short and specific. And mine existing data: performance records, help desk logs, assessment results. Existing data is usually the most objective evidence and the least used. Triangulate. If interviews say learners are confident and the error logs say they fail, believe the logs and ask where the confidence comes from.

## Slide 6: Context analysis: two contexts

- Performance context: where learners will use what they learn. Physical setting, social setting, managerial support, relevance to the job
- Learning context: where instruction happens. Facilities, equipment, bandwidth, LMS, scheduling constraints
- The closer the two, the better the transfer: simulation, practice environments, on-the-job components

Dick and Carey distinguish two contexts. The performance context is where learners will use what they learn: the physical setting, the social setting, the managerial support, and whether the new performance is expected and rewarded. If the performance context doesn't support the new behavior, training won't produce it. The learning context is where instruction happens: facilities, equipment, bandwidth, the LMS, and scheduling. The closer the two contexts, the better the transfer. That's why simulations, practice environments, and on-the-job components matter so much.

## Slide 7: From findings to implications

- Finding: a quarter of nurses have used the system before. Implication: a test-out or accelerated path
- Finding: night shift will not attend daytime classes. Implication: asynchronous options and on-unit support at night
- Finding: two devices per three nurses on the unit. Implication: short practice sessions at shared devices
- Every finding gets an implication, or it was not worth collecting

Analysis is only useful if it changes the design. For every finding, write an implication. A quarter of nurses have used the system before, so offer a test-out or accelerated path. Night shift won't attend daytime classes, so provide asynchronous options and on-unit support during night hours. Help desk calls concentrated in five workflows, so prioritize those for practice and build job aids for them. Two devices per three nurses on the unit, so design practice that fits short sessions at shared devices. Your implications table is the deliverable of assignment five, and the rest of the course builds on it.

## Slide 8: Common mistakes and your assignment

- Demographics only, with no implications
- Assuming homogeneity; most populations have subgroups
- Self-report alone
- Analyzing the learning context but not the performance context
- Assignment 5: two methods, real learners, every category, eight or more implications

Avoid the common mistakes: describing learners in demographics only, assuming everyone is the same, relying on self-report, and analyzing where the training happens while ignoring where the performance happens. For assignment five, use at least two methods, reach real learners even if the sample is small, address every category, analyze both contexts, and produce at least eight specific implications. If you cannot reach your learners at all, reconsider the project. A design built on imagined learners will not survive the later assignments.
