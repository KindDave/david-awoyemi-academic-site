Welcome to ID 553. ID 553 - Introduction Module. Presented by Idowu David Awoyemi, The University of Alabama.

## Slide 2: What this course is

- Instructional design: the systematic process of analyzing needs, designing and developing instruction, and evaluating whether it worked
- Eight modules following the ADDIE arc, treated critically
- Two case analyses and a complete instructional plan of your own

Welcome to ID 553, Introduction to the Principles of Instructional Design. I'm David Awoyemi. Instructional design is the systematic process of analyzing learning needs, designing and developing instruction, and evaluating whether it worked. Over eight modules we'll follow that arc, analysis, design, development, implementation, and evaluation, while questioning it along the way. You'll analyze two realistic design cases, and you'll produce a complete instructional plan for a real problem of your choosing.

## Slide 3: Who this course is for

- Teachers who want to design more deliberately
- Trainers and faculty developers who build courses and workshops
- Technologists and media specialists who work with subject-matter experts
- Graduate students preparing for advanced ID work or research
- No prior ID coursework assumed

This course is for teachers who want to design instruction more deliberately, for trainers and faculty developers who build courses and workshops, for technologists who work alongside subject-matter experts, and for graduate students heading toward advanced coursework or research. No prior instructional design coursework is assumed. What I do assume is that you'll bring a real instructional problem to work on.

## Slide 4: The design project runs through the course

- Choose one instructional problem in Module 1 and keep it
- Real, bounded, with learners you can talk to
- Assignments 5, 6, and 7 all apply to this one problem
- Too large: 'a course on leadership'. Right-sized: 'teaching new tutors to run a 30-minute writing conference'

In module one you'll choose an instructional problem and keep it for the whole course. Good problems are real, bounded, and involve learners you can actually talk to: a unit in your classroom, an onboarding topic at work, a skill your colleagues keep asking about. The learner analysis, the task analysis, and the instructional plan all apply to this one problem. Choosing something too large, like a course on leadership, makes every later assignment harder. Choosing something concrete makes them easier and more useful.

## Slide 5: The weekly rhythm

- Read the module pages and linked readings
- Check your understanding with the knowledge check (retakes allowed)
- Discuss by midweek; reply by the end of the module
- Produce the module assignment
- Plan on 6 to 8 hours per module

Each module follows the same pattern. Read the pages and any linked readings, and take notes in your own words. Check your understanding with a short, low-stakes quiz you can retake. Post in the discussion by midweek and reply to peers by the end of the module; the discussions are where the field's judgment calls get argued out, so treat them as seminar rather than a box to tick. And produce the assignment. Plan on six to eight hours per module.

- APA 7th edition; cite the readings

- One 48-hour extension on request; email idawoyemi@crimson.ua.edu

The course is worth eight hundred points. The two case analyses are two hundred, the learner and task analyses one hundred fifty, the instructional plan with its evaluation and reflection two hundred seventy-five, and the rest comes from the models assignment, the course reflection, and participation. Use APA seventh edition and cite the readings. You may use generative AI tools to brainstorm, outline, and check clarity, and you must disclose it; you may not submit AI-generated text as your analysis or reflection, because those are the evidence of your thinking. One forty-eight-hour extension is available on request. Post logistics questions in the pinned Questions thread, and email me for anything private. Let's get started.
