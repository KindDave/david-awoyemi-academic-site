## Title slide

Designing E-Learning: Multimedia, UDL, and Accessibility. ID 553 - Module 6: Creating Instruction. Presented by Idowu David Awoyemi, The University of Alabama.

## Slide 2: Three assumptions behind multimedia learning

- Two channels: words and pictures are processed separately
- Limited capacity in each channel
- Active processing: selecting, organizing, integrating
- Mayer's principles follow from these; they apply with most force for novices

Richard Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning rests on three assumptions. People process words and pictures in separate channels. Each channel has limited capacity. And learning requires active processing: selecting, organizing, and integrating. From decades of experiments, Mayer derived principles for combining words and pictures. They reduce extraneous load and direct attention, and they apply with the most force for novices. Experts can tolerate, and sometimes prefer, denser presentations.

## Slide 3: A screen before: three violations

- A slide about barcode scanning: a paragraph of text, a stock photo of a smiling nurse, a diagram of the workflow in the corner with its labels in a legend below
- Narration reads the paragraph word for word
- Background music plays throughout
- Violations: redundancy, coherence, spatial contiguity

Picture a screen from an e-learning module on barcode scanning. There's a paragraph of text, a stock photo of a smiling nurse, and a small workflow diagram in the corner with its labels in a legend underneath. The narration reads the paragraph word for word, and background music plays throughout. Three violations. Redundancy: identical words as narration and on-screen text overload the verbal channel. Coherence: the stock photo and the music are extraneous. Spatial contiguity: the labels sit in a legend instead of on the diagram, so the learner's eyes bounce back and forth.

## Slide 4: The same screen after

- The diagram fills the screen; labels sit on the steps they describe
- Narration explains the diagram; on-screen text is reduced to three signaled key terms
- Photo and music removed
- A caption toggle provides the narration as text for accessibility
- Principles applied: redundancy, coherence, spatial contiguity, signaling, modality

Now the same screen redesigned. The diagram fills the screen, and each label sits on the step it describes. The narration explains the diagram rather than reading a paragraph, and the on-screen text is reduced to three key terms highlighted at the moment the narration mentions them. The photo and the music are gone. A caption toggle provides the narration as text for anyone who needs it. Five principles applied: redundancy, coherence, spatial contiguity, signaling, and modality. Nothing was added; the design got better by removing things and putting the rest in the right place.

## Slide 5: More of Mayer's principles

- Temporal contiguity: words and pictures at the same time, not before or after
- Segmenting: learner-paced chunks with a continue control
- Pre-training: names and characteristics of components before the process
- Personalization: conversational style, 'you will see', not 'the learner will observe'
- Voice: a human voice over a machine voice; test synthetic voices before using them

A few more principles worth memorizing. Temporal contiguity: present corresponding words and pictures at the same time, so narration is synchronized with animation. Segmenting: present learner-paced chunks with a continue control rather than one long stream. Pre-training: teach the names and characteristics of key components before showing the process. Personalization: use a conversational style. And the voice principle: people learn better from a human voice than a machine voice. You may notice the narration you're hearing right now is synthetic. That's deliberate for a draft, and it's exactly the kind of thing you'd test with learners before shipping.

## Slide 6: Universal Design for Learning

- Design for the widest range of learners from the start, instead of retrofitting accommodations
- Multiple means of engagement: the why. Choice, relevance, clear goals and progress
- Multiple means of representation: the what. Captions, transcripts, alt text, glossaries, multiple examples
- Multiple means of action and expression: the how. Keyboard alternatives, choice of product format, planning templates
- Not three versions of everything; a set of checkpoints to consult during design

Universal Design for Learning, from CAST, is a framework for designing instruction that works for the widest range of learners from the start, instead of retrofitting accommodations afterward. Three principles. Multiple means of engagement, the why: choice within a task, relevance made explicit, clear goals and progress feedback. Multiple means of representation, the what: captions, transcripts, text alternatives for images, glossaries, multiple examples. Multiple means of action and expression, the how: alternatives to mouse-only interaction, choice of product format where the objective allows, planning templates. UDL is not 'make three versions of everything'. It's a set of checkpoints to consult during design so the most common barriers are removed before anyone hits them.

## Slide 7: Accessibility: the four WCAG principles

- Perceivable: text alternatives, captions and transcripts, 4.5 to 1 contrast, nothing conveyed by color alone
- Operable: everything works with a keyboard; visible focus; no seizure-inducing flashes; meaningful headings
- Understandable: language identified, predictable behavior, errors explained
- Robust: real headings, lists, and labels, not visual imitations
- Level AA is the standard in most institutional policy and in United States law for public entities

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines define what makes digital content usable by people with disabilities. Version 2.2 is current, and level double-A conformance is the standard in most institutional policies and in United States law for public entities. Four principles. Perceivable: text alternatives for images, captions and transcripts, sufficient color contrast, no information conveyed by color alone. Operable: everything works with a keyboard, focus is visible, nothing flashes dangerously, navigation is consistent. Understandable: the language is identified, behavior is predictable, errors are explained. Robust: use real headings, lists, and form labels rather than visual imitations, so assistive technologies can read the structure.

## Slide 8: The practical checklist and the screen reader test

- Every meaningful image has alt text; decorative images are marked decorative
- Every video has accurate captions; every audio file a transcript
- Real headings in logical order; contrast passes; links distinguishable without color
- Every interaction works with keyboard alone; drag-and-drop and timed interactions have alternatives
- Run a screen reader over the before and after screens: the before reads a paragraph, then 'image', then a table; the after reads the heading, the diagram's alt text, and three terms

Here's the checklist for every asset. Meaningful images have alt text and decorative ones are marked decorative. Videos have accurate captions and audio has transcripts. Headings are real headings in logical order. Contrast passes and links are distinguishable without color. Every interaction works with the keyboard alone, and drag-and-drop or timed interactions have alternatives. Documents pass their built-in accessibility checker. If you run a screen reader over our before screen, it reads a paragraph, then the word 'image', then a jumble of legend text. Over the after screen, it reads the heading, the diagram's alt text describing the workflow, and three key terms. Accessibility and Mayer's principles reinforce each other far more often than they conflict, and where they seem to, provide both: narration with captions the learner can toggle.

## Slide 9: Tools, standards, and your plan

- Authoring: Storyline, Rise, Captivate, iSpring. Open interactive: H5P. Video: Camtasia, OBS, Loom. Branching: Twine
- Generative AI for scripts, alt text, scenario variants, draft narration; verify and disclose
- SCORM and xAPI package modules for an LMS; Common Cartridge exchanges whole courses
- For your storyboarded lesson: list each media asset, the Mayer principles governing it, and the accessibility requirements that apply

Tool choice follows strategy and media decisions. Storyline is powerful and expensive; Rise is fast and constrained; H5P is free and good enough for most interactions. Camtasia, OBS, and Loom handle video. Twine prototypes branching. Generative AI can draft scripts, alt text, scenario variants, and, as you've heard, narration; verify everything and disclose its use. Modules reach an LMS as SCORM packages or xAPI statements, and whole courses travel between systems as Common Cartridge. For your instructional plan, list every media asset in the lesson you storyboard, name the Mayer principles that govern it, and note the accessibility requirements that apply. That list becomes part of assignment seven.
