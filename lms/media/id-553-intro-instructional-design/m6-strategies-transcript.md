## Slide 2: What an instructional strategy is

- The plan for getting learners from entry skills to the objectives
- Sequence, the events in each lesson, practice and feedback, grouping, media
- Placed after assessment so every choice can be checked against 'does this help learners perform on the assessment, and on the job?'

An instructional strategy is the plan for how learners get from their entry skills to the objectives: the sequence of content, the events in each lesson, the kinds of practice and feedback, the grouping, and the media. Dick and Carey place it after assessment, so every strategy decision can be checked against one question: does this help learners perform on the assessment, and therefore on the job? Three frameworks organize most of the decisions: Gagne's nine events for lesson structure, Merrill's principles for the whole, and the cognitive research for the details.

## Slide 3: A lesson mapped onto Gagne's nine events

- A 50-minute session I taught on barcode medication scanning
- 1 Attention: a real near-miss story. 2 Objective: stated. 3 Recall: 'How do you verify identity today?'
- 4 Present: a demonstration. 5 Guidance: two worked examples with alerts
- 6 Elicit performance: MISSING. 7 Feedback: MISSING
- 8 Assess: a quiz. 9 Retention and transfer: a job aid, but no spaced practice

Let me map a lesson I taught onto the nine events. Fifty minutes on barcode medication scanning. Event one, gain attention: I opened with a real near-miss story. Event two, I stated the objective. Event three, I asked how they verify identity today, recalling prior learning. Event four, I demonstrated the workflow. Event five, learning guidance: two worked examples with alerts. Then I went straight to event eight, a quiz. Events six and seven, eliciting performance and giving feedback, were missing entirely. Event nine, retention and transfer, was a job aid but no spaced practice. It was a presentation with a quiz, not instruction. The quiz scores were fine. The help desk calls a month later were not.

## Slide 4: What the missing events cost

- Without 6 and 7, learners never practiced under alerts; the first real alert happened on the ward
- Without varied, spaced practice (9), the procedure decayed before go-live
- The fix: cut the second worked example, add two practice scenarios with immediate feedback, and schedule a five-minute retrieval check a week later

What did the missing events cost? Without practice and feedback, learners never handled a mismatch alert under any pressure at all, so the first real alert happened on the ward. Without varied, spaced practice, the procedure decayed in the six weeks before go-live. The fix was simple: cut the second worked example, add two practice scenarios with immediate feedback, and schedule a five-minute retrieval check a week later. The events aren't a rigid script. A lesson may cycle through four to seven several times. But a lesson without six and seven is a presentation, and a lesson without nine rarely transfers.

## Slide 5: Merrill's First Principles as a strategy check

- Problem-centered: organized around real tasks, simple to complex; show the whole task early
- Activation: learners recall relevant experience; misconceptions surface
- Demonstration: the skill performed with the expert's reasoning visible
- Application: learners perform with coaching that fades; practice varied across contexts
- Integration: learners reflect, discuss, defend, adapt

Apply Merrill's five principles to the whole unit, not just each lesson. Problem-centered: is the instruction organized around a progression of real tasks rather than topics, and do learners see the whole task early even if they can't yet do it? Activation: do they recall relevant experience before new content, and do misconceptions surface? Demonstration: do they see the skill performed with the expert's reasoning made visible? Application: do they perform with coaching that fades, across varied contexts? Integration: do they reflect, discuss, defend, or adapt what they learned?

## Slide 6: Evidence-based practice design

- Retrieval practice beats rereading
- Spacing beats massing
- Interleaving problem types improves discrimination, even though it feels harder
- Worked examples first for novices, then fade to completion problems, then full problems
- Feedback: specific, timely, on the task and process, not the person
- Cognitive load: remove extraneous, manage intrinsic, free capacity for germane

The cognitive research is consistent. Retrieval practice strengthens retention far more than rereading, so build low-stakes retrieval into every lesson. Spaced practice beats massed practice, so return to earlier objectives in later lessons. Interleaving problem types improves discrimination and transfer, even though it feels harder to learners. Novices learn procedures faster from worked examples than from unguided problem solving; fade the examples into completion problems and then full problems. Feedback should be specific, timely, and focused on the task and the process rather than the person. And working memory is limited: remove extraneous load, manage intrinsic load by sequencing simple to complex, and use the freed capacity for self-explanation and comparison.

## Slide 7: Scaffolding and cognitive apprenticeship

- Scaffolding: support that lets learners perform beyond their unaided ability, withdrawn as competence grows
- Modeling: the expert performs and thinks aloud
- Coaching: guided practice with feedback
- Fading, then articulation, reflection, and exploration
- A strong default for complex skills; compatible with Gagne's events

Scaffolding is support that lets learners perform beyond their current unaided ability, withdrawn as competence grows. Cognitive apprenticeship names the moves. Modeling: the expert performs and thinks aloud. Coaching: guided practice with feedback. Scaffolding and fading. Articulation: learners explain their reasoning. Reflection: learners compare their process with the expert's. Exploration: learners frame and solve their own problems. That sequence is a strong default for complex skills, and it sits comfortably inside Gagne's events.

## Slide 8: Sequencing and grouping

- Procedures: in the order performed, with decisions taught where they occur
- Hierarchies: subordinate skills first, but show the whole task first so learners know why
- Concepts: defining attributes, then examples and non-examples, then classification practice
- Whole-task design (van Merrienboer): complete tasks from simple to complex, with information just in time
- Collaboration helps when the task benefits from multiple perspectives; hurts when it adds coordination load

Sequence by outcome type. Procedures go in the order performed, with decisions taught where they occur. Hierarchical skills go subordinate first, but show the whole task first so learners know why they're learning the parts. Concepts go defining attributes, then examples and non-examples of increasing subtlety, then classification practice. For complex skills, van Merrienboer's whole-task approach sequences a series of complete tasks from simple to complex with supportive information delivered just in time. Grouping follows the learner and context analysis: collaboration helps when the task benefits from multiple perspectives or articulation is a goal, and it hurts when it just adds coordination load.

## Slide 9: Media selection and your plan

- Choose media after strategy: what does event 4 or event 6 require?
- Clark: methods cause learning; media constrain which methods are feasible
- A simulation is worth building when the practice cannot be provided otherwise
- For each lesson in your plan: objectives, the nine events, practice and feedback design, grouping, media with a one-sentence justification
- Then check the whole plan against Merrill and cognitive load

Choose media after strategy, not before. The question isn't 'should we use video?' but 'what does the presentation event or the practice event require, and which medium supports it best at acceptable cost?' Richard Clark's long-standing argument is that media don't cause learning; methods do, and media constrain which methods are feasible. A simulation is worth building when the practice it enables can't be provided any other way. For your instructional plan, specify for each lesson the objectives covered, how each of the nine events is handled, the practice and feedback design, the grouping and delivery mode, and the media with a one-sentence justification tied to the event it serves. Then check the whole plan against Merrill's principles and against cognitive load.
