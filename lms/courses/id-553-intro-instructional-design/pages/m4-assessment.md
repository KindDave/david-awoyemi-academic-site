## Assessment comes before strategy

In Dick and Carey, assessment instruments are developed immediately after objectives and before instructional strategies. Backward Design makes the same move: decide what evidence you will accept before you plan activities. The reason is alignment. If the assessment is designed last, it tends to measure what was convenient to teach rather than what the objective required, and learners learn to the test rather than to the performance.

An assessment is aligned when the performance it elicits, the conditions it imposes, and the standard it applies match the objective. Check all three, every time.

## Types of assessment by purpose

| Type | When | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Entry-skill test | Before instruction | Confirm learners have the prerequisites; route those who do not |
| Pretest | Before instruction | Measure what learners already know of the objectives; allow test-out; provide a baseline |
| Practice tests and embedded checks | During instruction | Give learners feedback and the designer formative data; retrieval practice improves retention |
| Posttest | After instruction | Measure achievement of the objectives |
| Performance assessment | After instruction, or on the job | Measure the actual performance under realistic conditions |

Not every project needs all five. Every project needs the last two, and most benefit from embedded checks.

## Criterion-referenced, not norm-referenced

Instructional design uses **criterion-referenced** assessment: each learner is measured against the standard in the objective, not against other learners. Grading on a curve makes no sense when the question is whether a nurse can administer medication safely. The criterion in the objective is the passing standard; if learners fail in large numbers, the instruction is the problem, not the learners.

## Matching item types to objectives

| Objective type | Appropriate assessment | Inappropriate assessment |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Verbal information (state, list, describe) | Short answer, matching, multiple choice, completion | Extended performance tasks |
| Intellectual skill: discrimination and concept classification | Classify new examples and non-examples; multiple choice with well-designed distractors | Recall of the definition alone |
| Intellectual skill: rule use and problem solving | Solve new problems; scenario-based items; worked cases with a decision | Items that repeat examples used in instruction |
| Motor skill | Observed performance with a checklist | Written description of the procedure |
| Attitude | Observed choice in realistic situations; behavioral records over time; self-report as supplement only | A quiz on what the right attitude is |
| Create-level cognitive objectives | Products judged with a rubric | Multiple choice |

The most common alignment error is testing a rule-use objective with a recall item. If the objective says "decide," the assessment must present a decision.

## Writing good selected-response items

- Stem states a complete problem; the learner should be able to answer before reading the options.
- Options are parallel in length and grammar; the correct answer is not the longest.
- Distractors are plausible and, ideally, drawn from the common errors identified in the task analysis. A distractor no one chooses is wasted.
- Avoid "all of the above," "none of the above," and negatives in the stem.
- One item, one objective. Tag each item with the objective number so you can analyze results by objective.

## Performance assessment and rubrics

When the objective is a performance or product, the assessment is a task plus a scoring guide.

**Checklists** list the required steps or features; each is present or absent. Use for procedures with a correct sequence.

**Rating scales** score each criterion on a scale (for example, 1 to 4) without describing the levels. Fast, but unreliable across raters.

**Analytic rubrics** describe each criterion at each level of performance. They are the most reliable and give the most useful feedback. Every assignment rubric in this course is analytic. Build one by:

1. Listing the criteria from the objective and task analysis (three to six is typical).
2. Describing the exemplary level for each criterion in observable terms.
3. Describing the beginning level.
4. Filling in the intermediate levels by naming what is present and what is missing.
5. Testing the rubric on two or three real or simulated samples with a second rater, and revising where you disagree.

**Holistic rubrics** give one overall score with a description per level. Faster to apply, less diagnostic. Use for large-scale screening, not for feedback.

## Feedback

Assessment during instruction is only formative if the learner gets feedback they can use. Effective feedback is specific to the criterion, tells the learner what to do next, and arrives while the learner can still act on it. Automated feedback in quizzes should explain why the correct answer is correct and why the chosen distractor is wrong; the knowledge checks in this course model that pattern.

## Validity, reliability, and fairness

- **Validity**: the assessment measures the objective. Alignment is the main validity check for instructional assessment.
- **Reliability**: the assessment gives consistent results across items, occasions, and raters. Enough items per objective, clear rubrics, and rater training are the levers.
- **Fairness**: the assessment does not disadvantage learners for reasons unrelated to the objective (reading load, cultural references, inaccessible formats). Apply the UDL principle of multiple means of action and expression where the objective allows.
