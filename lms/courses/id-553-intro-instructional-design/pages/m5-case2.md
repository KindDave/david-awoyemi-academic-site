*This case is fictional. Any resemblance to real organizations or people is coincidental.*

## The request

Pinewood Community College enrolls about 9,400 students across a main campus, two satellite centers, and a large online program. It employs 118 full-time faculty and roughly 310 adjuncts. In August, the Vice President for Academic Affairs, Dr. Renee Castillo, announced an "AI Literacy Initiative" with three goals: every course syllabus would include an AI-use policy by January; every faculty member would complete "AI literacy professional development" by May; and the college would report to its accreditor, in the spring, on how it was "preparing students for an AI-influenced workforce."

Jordan Lee, the college's instructional designer in the Center for Teaching and Learning (CTL), was asked to design the professional development. The CTL has three staff: Jordan, a director named Priya Natarajan who also teaches two sections of English each term, and a part-time technologist who supports the LMS. The initiative has a $40,000 budget for the year, most of which Dr. Castillo expects to spend on "a platform or a partner."

## What Jordan learned

**Faculty survey (September, 241 responses of 428, 56%).** Asked to describe their stance on generative AI in their courses:

| Stance | Full-time | Adjunct |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ban it; it is cheating | 31% | 38% |
| Allow with restrictions | 44% | 35% |
| Encourage and teach it | 18% | 12% |
| Have not thought about it | 7% | 15% |

Asked what they wanted from professional development, the top responses were "how to detect AI cheating" (63%), "how to write an AI policy" (58%), "examples from my discipline" (51%), and "how to use AI to save time on grading and prep" (47%). Only 22% selected "how to teach students to use AI well." Free-text responses included "I am not going to learn a tool that will be replaced next year," "I have 180 students and no time," and "Just tell me what the college policy is and I'll put it in the syllabus."

**Focus groups (three, 24 faculty).** Themes: fear of being blamed for student misconduct; resentment of past mandatory training ("the last one was a 45-minute video with a quiz that anyone could pass by guessing"); real curiosity among a minority who had experimented; frustration among nursing, welding, and automotive faculty that the initiative seemed aimed at "writing classes"; and strong doubt that AI detection tools work. Two adjuncts said they teach at three colleges and could not attend anything synchronous.

**Students.** A student government survey (n = 612) found 71% used generative AI for coursework at least monthly; 48% said they were unsure whether their use was allowed; 29% had received contradictory instructions from different instructors in the same term.

**Existing resources.** The library had run four well-attended "AI 101" webinars. Three faculty (in English, business, and computer information systems) had already redesigned assignments around AI and were willing to share. The CTL's LMS could host self-paced modules with completion tracking. A vendor had pitched a "turnkey AI literacy course for faculty" (12 hours of video modules, generic across disciplines, $28,000 per year, completion certificates included). Dr. Castillo liked that it "solves the reporting problem."

**Accreditation.** The accreditor's request was open-ended: describe what the college is doing to prepare students for the workforce, including with respect to emerging technologies. There was no requirement that every faculty member complete anything.

**Governance.** The Faculty Senate had formed an AI committee that met twice and produced no recommendations. Its chair told Jordan, "If Academic Affairs mandates a training, we will fight it. If you ask us to help design it, we will."

## The complication

In October, a well-publicized incident occurred: an adjunct instructor accused nine students of AI-generated work based on a detection tool's score; three of the accusations were later withdrawn after the students produced drafts and version histories. The college newspaper ran the story. Dr. Castillo emailed Jordan: "This is exactly why we need everyone trained, and soon. Can we move the faculty completion deadline to March? I would like to see a plan by the end of next week. The vendor option is still on the table and honestly it would be simplest."

Jordan has $40,000, a part-time technologist, a director with a full teaching load, a divided faculty, three willing champions, a hostile-but-persuadable Senate, a vendor, and eight days to produce a plan.

## Questions to consider

Use the six-step framework. Additional prompts specific to this case:

- What is the performance gap? Whose performance? Is it primarily a knowledge and skill gap, or something else?
- The faculty asked for detection and policy; the initiative's stated goal is student preparation. How do you reconcile what learners want with what the organization needs, and with what the evidence supports?
- What are the risks of the vendor course, beyond cost? What are its genuine advantages?
- How would you handle the difference between full-time and adjunct faculty, and among disciplines?
- What role could the three champions, the library, and the Senate committee play, and what would it cost to involve them?
- What evidence would satisfy the accreditor, and does it require universal completion?
- How does the October incident change the priorities, if at all?
- What would you tell Dr. Castillo about the March deadline?
