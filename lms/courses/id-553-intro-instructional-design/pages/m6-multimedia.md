## Multimedia learning: Mayer's principles

Richard Mayer's cognitive theory of multimedia learning rests on three assumptions: people process words and pictures in separate channels; each channel has limited capacity; and learning requires active processing (selecting, organizing, integrating). From decades of experiments Mayer derived principles for designing instruction that combines words and pictures. The most useful for practice:

| Principle | Guidance | Design implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Multimedia | People learn better from words and pictures than from words alone | Add a relevant diagram, not a decorative photo |
| Coherence | Exclude extraneous words, pictures, and sounds | Cut background music, tangential anecdotes, decorative clip art |
| Signaling | Highlight the organization of essential material | Headings, arrows, bold key terms, an outline that persists |
| Redundancy | Do not present the same words as on-screen text and narration | Narrate a diagram; do not narrate a slide of text |
| Spatial contiguity | Place words near the pictures they describe | Labels on the diagram, not in a legend across the page |
| Temporal contiguity | Present corresponding words and pictures at the same time | Narration synchronized with animation, not before or after |
| Segmenting | Present in learner-paced segments rather than a continuous unit | Short chunks with a "continue" control |
| Pre-training | Teach the names and characteristics of key components first | A short glossary or component tour before the process animation |
| Modality | Present words as narration rather than on-screen text when paired with graphics | Narrated animation beats captioned animation for novices (with a caption option for accessibility) |
| Personalization | Use conversational rather than formal style | "You will see..." rather than "The learner will observe..." |
| Voice | Use a human voice rather than a machine voice | Applies to narration; test synthetic voices before using them |

These principles reduce extraneous cognitive load and direct attention. They apply to slides, videos, e-learning modules, job aids, and web pages. They apply with more force for novices; experts can tolerate, and sometimes prefer, denser presentations.

## Universal Design for Learning

UDL (CAST) is a framework for designing instruction that works for the widest range of learners from the start, instead of retrofitting accommodations. Its three principles map onto the affective, recognition, and strategic networks of the brain:

1. **Multiple means of engagement** (the "why"): options for recruiting interest, sustaining effort, and self-regulation. Examples: choice of topic within a task, relevance made explicit, clear goals and progress feedback.
2. **Multiple means of representation** (the "what"): options for perception, language, and comprehension. Examples: captions and transcripts, text alternatives for images, glossaries, multiple examples, background knowledge supports.
3. **Multiple means of action and expression** (the "how"): options for physical action, expression, and executive function. Examples: alternatives to mouse-only interaction, choice of product format where the objective allows, planning templates, checklists.

UDL is not "make three versions of everything." It is a set of checkpoints to consult during design so that the most common barriers are removed before anyone hits them. In your instructional plan you will pick two or three checkpoints and show how the design addresses them.

## Accessibility: WCAG

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) define what makes digital content usable by people with disabilities. Version 2.2 is current; conformance at level AA is the standard in most institutional policies and in United States law for public entities. The four principles:

- **Perceivable.** Text alternatives for images; captions and transcripts for audio and video; content that can be presented in different ways without losing meaning; sufficient color contrast (4.5:1 for normal text); no information conveyed by color alone.
- **Operable.** Everything works with a keyboard; users have enough time; nothing flashes in ways that cause seizures; navigation is consistent and headings are meaningful.
- **Understandable.** Language is identified; behavior is predictable; input errors are identified and explained.
- **Robust.** Content works with current and future assistive technologies; use real headings, lists, and form labels rather than visual imitations.

Practical checklist for every e-learning asset:

1. Every meaningful image has alt text; decorative images are marked decorative.
2. Every video has accurate captions; every audio file has a transcript.
3. Headings are real headings in a logical order.
4. Color contrast passes; links are distinguishable without color.
5. All interactions work with keyboard alone; focus is visible.
6. Documents (PDF, Word, PowerPoint) are tagged and pass the built-in accessibility checker.
7. Any drag-and-drop, timed, or motion-based interaction has an alternative.

Accessibility and Mayer's principles reinforce each other more often than they conflict. Where they seem to (modality says narrate, accessibility says caption), provide both: narration with captions the learner can toggle.

## Tools for e-learning development

| Category | Examples | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authoring tools | Articulate Storyline 360, Articulate Rise, Adobe Captivate, iSpring | Interactive modules exported as SCORM or xAPI for an LMS |
| Open interactive content | H5P | Interactive video, branching scenarios, quizzes; free and embeds in most LMSs |
| Video | Camtasia, OBS Studio, Loom, Panopto | Screencasts and lectures; add captions |
| Graphics and layout | Canva, Figma, PowerPoint | Diagrams, job aids, storyboards |
| Simulation and branching | Storyline, Twine, H5P Branching Scenario | Decision practice with consequences |
| Generative AI | Text, image, and voice models | Drafting scripts, alt text, scenario variants, and synthetic narration; verify everything and disclose use |

Tool choice follows strategy and media decisions. Storyline is powerful and expensive; Rise is fast and constrained; H5P is free and good enough for most interactions. Match the tool to the interaction the strategy requires, the skills of your team, and the budget.

## Standards for packaging

Modules built in authoring tools are delivered to an LMS as **SCORM** packages (older, widely supported, tracks completion and score) or **xAPI** statements (newer, tracks richer activity, needs a learning record store). Course-level content is exchanged among LMSs as **IMS Common Cartridge**. Knowing these names lets you talk to LMS administrators and vendors.
