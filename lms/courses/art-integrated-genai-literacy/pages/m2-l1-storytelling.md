## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. Write a scene brief that specifies characters, objective, obstacle, setting, tone, and form.
2. Use a text model to draft a scene and then direct at least two rounds of revision with specific dramaturgical notes.
3. Rewrite at least a third of the dialogue in your own voice and explain what you changed and why.
4. Prepare the scene for a table read.

## Warm-up: the smallest scene (5 minutes)

Write three lines of dialogue between two characters who want opposite things. Do not name the things; let the wanting show in the lines. This is the core of dramatic writing: **objective** meets **obstacle**. A text model can produce fluent dialogue instantly, but it does not know what your characters want unless you tell it.

## Key ideas

### The scene brief

A text model is a fast, tireless, slightly bland writing partner. Its first draft will be competent and forgettable. What makes it useful is a brief that carries your artistic choices. Use this frame:

| Element | Question | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Characters | Who, how old, what relationship? | "A retiring band director and a first-year teacher taking over her program" |
| Objective | What does each want in this scene? | "She wants him to keep the jazz band; he wants to cut it for budget" |
| Obstacle | What stands in the way? | "The principal has already approved the cut; she does not know that yet" |
| Setting | Where and when? | "Band room, last day of school, instruments half packed" |
| Tone | How should it feel? | "Warm, wry, one moment of real anger, no tidy resolution" |
| Form | How long, what shape? | "Two pages, one location, ends on a decision not spoken aloud" |

### Prompting is directing

After the first draft, your prompts become director's notes. Good notes are specific and actionable: "Cut the exposition in the first six lines; start with her already holding the trumpet case." "Give him one line where he almost agrees, then retreats." "Replace the final speech with a single gesture." Vague notes ("make it better," "more emotional") produce vague rewrites.

### The human rewrite

A scene is finished when it sounds like people you know. Models default to characters who say what they mean and explain their feelings. Real people deflect, interrupt, joke at the wrong moment, and leave things unsaid. Your rewrite is where the scene becomes yours: cut explanation, add subtext, and give each character one verbal habit.

## Activity: draft, direct, rewrite (55 minutes)

1. **Write the brief** using the six-element frame. Choose a situation connected to a school, a rehearsal, a family, or a community you know.
2. **Draft.** Paste the brief into a text model with this instruction: "Write a two-page, two-character scene in standard stage-play format based on this brief. Use only dialogue and brief stage directions."
3. **Read it aloud** (both parts). Mark every line that explains a feeling instead of showing it, and every line that no real person would say.
4. **Direct round 1.** Give the model three specific notes drawn from your marks. Ask for a revised draft.
5. **Direct round 2.** Give three more notes. Focus on the ending and on subtext.
6. **Rewrite by hand.** Copy the scene into a document. Rewrite at least one third of the lines yourself. Track your changes or highlight them.
7. **Prepare for a table read.** Add a title, a character list with one-line descriptions, and a one-sentence note to actors about tone.
8. **Save** as PDF named `Lesson-2-1_LastName_Title.pdf`.

### Note bank

Director's notes you can adapt for revision rounds:

- "Start the scene later: cut the first [n] lines."
- "Give [character] a line they regret immediately."
- "Replace every line that names an emotion with an action."
- "Add one interruption where they talk over each other."
- "End on an image, not a statement."
- "Make the setting do work: have a prop from the setting change hands."

## Reflection prompts

1. Where did the model's draft surprise you, and where was it predictable?
2. Which of your rewrites would you defend to a student who preferred the model's version?
3. How would you structure this activity so students cannot skip the rewrite step?
