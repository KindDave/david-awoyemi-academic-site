## Learning objectives

By the end of this lesson you will be able to:

1. Decompose a beat into layers (kick, snare, hi-hat, bass, chords, melody) and describe each as a repeating pattern.
2. Build a 16-bar beat in a loop-based digital audio workstation using pattern, repetition, and variation.
3. Use an AI-assisted feature (loop recommendation, pattern generation, or automatic mastering) and explain what it decided for you.
4. Give a peer structured feedback on the arrangement of their beat.

## Warm-up: clap the pattern (5 minutes)

Clap this pattern four times: **1 (rest) and (rest) 3 (rest) and (rest)**. Now say "kick" on 1 and 3 and "snare" on 2 and 4 while clapping eighth notes with your other hand. You just held three simultaneous patterns in your head. A beat maker does the same thing, but writes each pattern down so a machine can repeat it exactly.

## Key ideas

### Beats are patterns, and patterns are code

Computational thinking has four moves: **decomposition** (break the problem into parts), **pattern recognition** (find the repeats), **abstraction** (keep the essential, drop the rest), and **algorithm design** (write the steps). A drum loop is all four at once. You decompose the groove into instruments, notice that the hi-hat repeats every eighth note, abstract the groove into a grid, and sequence bars into a form.

### The grid

Loop-based tools such as Soundtrap show time as a grid: bars across the top, instruments down the side. One bar of 4/4 has 16 sixteenth-note cells. A kick on cells 1 and 9, a snare on cells 5 and 13, and hi-hats on every odd cell is the most common pattern in popular music. Everything else is variation on it.

### Form is repetition with variation

A 16-bar beat is not sixteen different bars. It is usually one four-bar phrase repeated four times with small changes: a fill in bar 4, a dropped kick in bar 8, a new element at bar 9, a pause before bar 13. Listeners hear the repetition as stability and the variation as interest. Sequencing those changes is algorithm design.

### Where the AI is

Modern workstations embed AI in several places: loop recommendation (the tool suggests loops that fit your key and tempo), pattern generation (the tool proposes a drum pattern in a chosen style), and automatic mastering (the tool balances levels and loudness). Each is a decision made for you. Your job in this lesson is to use at least one, notice what it decided, and decide whether you agree.

## Activity: build a 16-bar beat (50 minutes)

1. **Set up.** Open a new Soundtrap project. Set the tempo (try 90 BPM for hip hop, 120 for house, 105 for Afrobeats) and choose a key.
2. **Layer 1: drums.** Add a drum track. Using the pattern editor or a loop, build a four-bar drum pattern. Write the pattern on paper first as a grid of Xs; that is your decomposition.
3. **Layer 2: bass.** Add a bass loop or play a simple two-note bassline that repeats every bar.
4. **Layer 3: chords or melody.** Use the tool's loop recommendation to find a chord or melody loop in your key. Note what it recommended and why you accepted or rejected it.
5. **Extend to 16 bars.** Duplicate the four-bar phrase three times.
6. **Add variation.** Make at least four deliberate changes: a fill at the end of bar 4, remove the bass in bar 8, introduce a new element at bar 9, and create a one-beat silence before bar 13. Write these changes as a numbered list; that list is your algorithm.
7. **Use one AI feature.** Try automatic mastering or pattern generation. Listen with and without it. Record what changed.
8. **Export** as MP3 and name it `Lesson-1-2_LastName_Title.mp3`.
